## How the built deck differs from the original brief

The original brief is kept in git history. Where the deck departs from it:

- **TOP500 dropped.** The brief asked for a TOP500 image against edge computing; the slide now uses xAI's Colossus with two edge perception settings, and the notes warn against substituting TOP500 counts for AI compute capacity.
- **The 20 W brain figure is not drawn.** It survives as spoken context in the notes; a bare whole-brain number reads as a matched-workload comparison with a processor.
- **Slide order.** The policy slide was moved to the end of the original ten and carried 朱祥维's quotation as the closing line; the analog slide precedes it. On 23 September 2026 the deck was extended with nine further slides, so the policy slide is no longer the last page — the quotation now closes the first half, and the deck ends on the reference gathering deck's image. The note on that slide was rewritten to say so, and the cross-reference on the brain slide was shortened to "the policy slide".
- **Sources added.** The brief specified neither the Pilz et al. compute-share study nor the Moore 1965 paper; both were added to anchor the strategic and post-Moore claims. The two memristor papers on slide 13 were added at the speaker's direction, with DOIs.
- **Figures redrawn.** The brief asked for figures on several slides without specifying them; the von Neumann, edge, synaptic, in-memory and tolerance figures are this deck's own drawings, generated by `scripts/build-diagrams.py`.
- **The brief's advantage/disadvantage pair reassigned.** Slide 11's raw note pairs "leverage the chip area and mature process" with "low density" as if they were one device's. They are not: they belong to the two columns. Low density is the SRAM limitation and is carried on the SRAM side; the DRAM column carries its own trade (coarser arithmetic, destructive read). The takeaway keeps the brief's pairing in compressed form.
- **"Training and inference necessarily separate" softened.** The brief's 训推分离 note says 必然. The slide says "in practice". A device that trains on-chip is conceivable; what is observable today is that training and inference are done separately. The note records the weaker claim.
- **FPMA is this deck's coinage.** The brief asks for the FPGA/memristor-crossbar comparison. The name FPMA (field-programmable memristor array) is the speaker's, introduced on slide 14; it is not an established term in the literature and the note says so.
- **Slide 17's framework figure not reproduced.** The reference deck's KANalogue figure remains in Chinese. Rather than ship a half-translated panel, this deck draws its own English schematic of the function-basis idea (`media/diagrams/device-function-basis.svg`) and copies the framework PDF for the credit line to link.

## Other claims

- [Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report): converging U.S.–China model performance supports the algorithms discussion, but does not establish training-data parity.
- [Pilz et al., Trends in AI Supercomputers (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16026): leading AI supercomputer performance doubled every nine months between 2019 and 2025 (2.5× per year, 90% CI 2.4–2.7), from 1.6× per year more chips and 1.6× per year better chips. The leading system in March 2025, xAI's Colossus, used 200,000 NVIDIA H100/H200 chips, about 300 MW and roughly $7B of hardware, over 50× Summit in 2019. The largest public system, El Capitan, is about 22% of Colossus. A regression on leading systems in a ~500-system dataset, not global capacity, not a forecast, and not a TOP500 measure.
- [Moore's 1965 paper](https://download.intel.com/newsroom/2023/manufacturing/moores-law-electronics.pdf): approximately annual doubling of components at minimum component cost. The two-year revision is later; neither statement is an annual performance-per-watt guarantee.
- [NVIDIA RTX 5090 specifications](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/graphics-cards/50-series/rtx-5090/): correct product name and 32 GB GDDR7. The brief's “GTX” naming is corrected.
- [Spike-based dynamic computing, Nature Communications (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-47811-6): context for the approximate 20 W brain-power figure. The figure is no longer drawn on the slide it once appeared on; it survives as spoken context in the notes. Not a matched-workload comparison with a GPU.
- [Le Gallo et al., 64-core mixed-signal AIMC (2023)](https://research.ibm.com/publications/a-64-core-mixed-signal-in-memory-compute-chip-based-on-phase-change-memory-for-deep-neural-network-inference): published evidence for in-memory inference potential on specified tasks. No longer printed on the brain slide; recorded here.
- [Rasch et al., hardware-aware training (2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-40770-4): mitigation of hardware nonidealities, not blanket immunity to analog errors.
- [Chua, "Memristor — the missing circuit element", IEEE Transactions on Circuit Theory 18(5), 507–519 (1971)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCT.1971.1083337): the fourth element is predicted from the symmetry of the two-terminal element relations, not from a measured device. The prediction is a definitional argument about circuit variables; it is not a materials result.
- [Strukov, Snider, Stewart and Williams, "The missing memristor found", Nature 453, 80–83 (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06932): a TiO₂ thin-film device whose resistance depends on the charge that has passed through it. The slide's framing — predicted in 1971, reported in 2008, thirty-seven years apart — is the chronology of those two publications, nothing more.
- **Memristor versus memristive systems.** The 2008 identification is contested: the reported device is one of Chua and Kang's broader *memristive systems* (1976), which need not satisfy the ideal memristor's constitutive relation. The slide's takeaway, "the device remembers — its resistance depends on the charge that has passed through it", is written to be true of the broad class. Do not upgrade it to a claim that an ideal memristor has been realised.
- **Crossbar arithmetic is shown idealised.** Slide 12 draws Ohm's law at each crossing and Kirchhoff's current law at each column as if the wires were ideal. A real array has wire resistance, IR drop and sneak paths, and the departure from the ideal is what the analog-error literature is about. The figure is the model, not the hardware.
- **In-array computing (slide 11).** The SRAM and DRAM figures are schematics; no array size, cell count or process node is claimed anywhere. "Leverage the chip area and mature process" is the reason the routes are attractive, not a measured advantage. The DRAM column's "coarser arithmetic" refers to the small number of bits per cell and the destructive read, not to an accuracy measurement.
- **The FPGA/FPMA comparison is an analogy.** Slide 14 compares a memristor array to an FPGA: both are a regular fabric whose function is set by configuring its cells. It is not a claim about maturity, performance or tooling — the FPGA has fifty years of design infrastructure behind it and the memristor array does not, and the note says so. FPMA is the speaker's coinage; see above.
- **Training and inference separated "in practice".** The observable pattern, not a law of nature. On-device learning exists in the literature; the slide's argument is that the inference workloads the deck is aimed at do not rewrite their weights, so a device optimised for inference can treat the weights as fixed.
- **The memory-first/compute-first framing is ours.** Slide 16's two routes are the speaker's way of describing a design stance, not a taxonomy from the literature. The claim it carries is narrow: if the weights never change, starting from the operation and choosing physics to perform it is available, and the array becomes an implementation choice.
- **KANalogue caveats (carried from the reference deck, slide 17).** The reference deck's own qualifications travel with the material: the baseline the framework compares against is a linear array with ADC–digital-nonlinearity–DAC conversion, so the deck must not claim a converter-free machine; and a reported node efficiency is not a TOPS/W figure. The framework figure itself is not reproduced — see above.
- **The three application settings (slide 18) are proposed categories.** Autofocus, guidance, and touch and soft robotic arms are the reference deck's three settings, translated. The guidance panel carries the reference deck's own warning printed on the figure: volume and high end are proposed positions, not verified market sizes or performance. No market size, unit volume or order-of-value is claimed anywhere on the slide.
